## How to use this file

Grounded, real-world facts for each topic listed in `bible/world.md` → Topics, meant to keep episodes culturally and factually accurate. These are starting reference points, not exhaustive — verify specific numbers or dates before locking them into a script.

## Lucha libre

- Arena México, in Colonia Doctores, opened in 1933 and is nicknamed "La Catedral de la Lucha Libre" (the Cathedral of Lucha Libre).
- CMLL (Consejo Mundial de Lucha Libre), founded in 1933, is Mexico's oldest lucha libre promotion.
- Masks (máscaras) are central to a luchador's identity; some wrestlers keep their real identity secret for their entire careers.
- "Luchas de apuestas" are high-stakes matches where the loser must remove their mask (or, in "cabellera" matches, have their hair shaved) as the wager.
- El Santo, one of the most famous masked luchadores in history, became a folk hero and starred in dozens of films without ever publicly unmasking during his career.
- Matches contrast técnicos (fan-favorite "good guys") against rudos ("bad guys"), with storytelling as central as athleticism.

## The Metro

- Mexico City's Metro (Sistema de Transporte Colectivo) opened in 1969.
- Each station has its own pictogram/icon in addition to its name — originally designed to help riders who couldn't read navigate the system.
- It's one of the busiest metro systems in the Americas, historically with very low, flat fares.
- Construction has repeatedly uncovered archaeological finds; a small Aztec temple to the wind god Ehécatl was found while building Pino Suárez station and is preserved inside it.

## Mexican food

- Traditional Mexican cuisine was inscribed by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010.
- Tacos al pastor — pork marinated in achiote and dried chiles, cooked on a vertical spit — evolved from shawarma techniques brought by Lebanese immigrants to central Mexico in the 20th century.
- Nixtamalization — soaking and cooking corn in an alkaline solution (traditionally limewater) — is the ancient technique that makes corn usable for tortillas and masa, and improves its nutritional value.
- Mexico City street food staples include tacos, quesadillas, elotes and esquites (corn), tamales, and tortas.

## Abuela's cooking

- Family recipes in Mexico are traditionally passed down orally and by hand across generations rather than written out.
- Classic homestyle dishes include mole, pozole, sopa de fideo, frijoles de la olla, arroz rojo, quesadillas, and sopes.
- The molcajete, a mortar carved from volcanic stone, is still widely used for hand-made salsas.
- La comida (the main family meal, usually eaten mid-afternoon) is a significant weekly tradition, especially on Sundays.

## The Aztecs/Mexica

- According to tradition, the Mexica founded their capital, Tenochtitlán, around 1325 CE on an island in Lake Texcoco after seeing an eagle perched on a cactus eating a serpent — the origin of the image on Mexico's flag and coat of arms.
- The Templo Mayor was Tenochtitlán's main temple, dedicated to Huitzilopochtli (war/sun) and Tlaloc (rain). Its ruins were rediscovered by utility workers in downtown Mexico City in 1978 and are now an open-air museum site.
- Modern Mexico City is built largely atop the drained lakebed of what was once Lake Texcoco.

## Local animals

- The axolotl (ajolote) is a critically endangered salamander native to Xochimilco's canals, known for being able to regenerate lost limbs and for retaining its larval features into adulthood (neoteny).
- The Xoloitzcuintle (Xolo) is an ancient hairless dog breed native to Mexico, considered sacred by the Aztecs and traditionally believed to guide the souls of the dead through the underworld.
- Street dogs are a common sight across Mexico City neighborhoods, and the city has various stray-dog welfare and adoption initiatives.

## Volcanic geography

- Mexico City sits in the Valley of Mexico at roughly 2,240 m (7,350 ft) elevation, surrounded by mountains and volcanoes.
- Popocatépetl (still active) and Iztaccíhuatl are the two most famous volcanoes visible from the valley on clear days.
- The valley's ancient lake system — including Lake Texcoco — formed in a basin shaped in part by surrounding volcanic activity.

## Water in CDMX

- Mexico City is gradually sinking (subsidence) because it's built on a former lakebed and has long relied on over-extracting groundwater from the aquifer beneath it — some areas sink several centimeters per year.
- The Aztecs managed the valley's lake system with dikes, causeways, and aqueducts, including one bringing fresh spring water from Chapultepec.
- A significant share of the city's modern water supply comes from the Cutzamala System, which pumps water uphill from outside the valley.
- Aging pipes across the city lose a substantial amount of water to leaks before it reaches homes.

## Markets

- Mercados are permanent, roofed markets with fixed stalls, typically one serving each neighborhood.
- Tianguis are temporary open-air markets that rotate locations by day of the week; the word comes from the Nahuatl "tianquiztli."
- La Merced is one of the oldest and largest traditional markets in Mexico City.

## Mexican myths

- La Llorona — the legend of a weeping woman's ghost, said to wander at night mourning children she lost — is one of Mexico's most widespread folk legends.
- The story of Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl tells of a warrior and a princess whose tragic love explains the origin of the two volcanoes.
- Quetzalcóatl, the feathered serpent, is one of the central deities of Mexica mythology.

## Local architecture

- Vecindades are traditional shared-courtyard tenement buildings, historically housing multiple working-class families around a central patio — common in older, central Mexico City neighborhoods.
- Many colonial-era buildings in the historic center were built with tezontle, a reddish volcanic stone.
- La Casa Azul, in Coyoacán, is the cobalt-blue house where Frida Kahlo lived and is now a museum dedicated to her.
- Because the city sits on soft former lakebed soil, many historic buildings settle unevenly over time, leaving visibly tilted floors and walls.

## Día de Muertos

- Celebrated November 1 (honoring deceased children, "angelitos") and November 2 (honoring deceased adults).
- UNESCO inscribed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008.
- Ofrendas (home altars) typically include cempasúchil (marigold) flowers, papel picado, pan de muerto, sugar skulls (calaveras de azúcar), photos, and the favorite food/drink of the deceased.
- Cempasúchil petals and their scent are traditionally believed to guide spirits back home.
- Mexico City's large Día de Muertos parade began in 2016, inspired in part by a fictional parade shown in the James Bond film *Spectre* — the city then staged a real one, which has continued since.

## Chinampas

- Chinampas are rectangular plots built up from lake mud and vegetation in shallow lakebeds, anchored by ahuejote (willow) trees planted along the edges.
- The Aztecs/Mexica used chinampas extensively to grow food for Tenochtitlán, their island capital.
- Xochimilco preserves a living, still-farmed remnant of this system and, along with the historic center of Mexico City, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.
- Trajineras — colorful flat-bottomed boats, each traditionally painted with a woman's name — now carry mostly visitors along the canals rather than produce.
